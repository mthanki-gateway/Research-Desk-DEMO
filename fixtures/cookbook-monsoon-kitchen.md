# The Monsoon Kitchen — recipes for wet months

## Why these recipes

Everything here was worked out during rainy seasons in a kitchen with two
burners, no oven, and unreliable power. The constraints shaped the food more
than any tradition did: nothing takes more than one pot, nothing needs an
accurate temperature, and every recipe survives being abandoned halfway
through when the lights go out.

Quantities feed four unless stated. Salt is always to taste and the amounts
given are a starting point rather than an instruction.

## Kanji rice water

The simplest thing in this book and the one people ask about most.

Boil one cup of red rice in six cups of water until the grains split open,
roughly forty minutes. Do not drain. Salt it, add a spoon of coconut oil, and
eat the whole thing — liquid and grain together — from a deep bowl.

Served warm it is breakfast. Served cool it is what you drink on a day too
humid to eat. The starch in the cooking water is the point; a recipe that
tells you to drain it has misunderstood the dish entirely.

## Tamarind and drumstick stew

Soak a lime-sized ball of tamarind in a cup of warm water for ten minutes,
then squeeze it out and discard the solids. Cut two drumsticks into
finger-lengths. Simmer them in the tamarind water with a teaspoon of turmeric
and two slit green chillies until the flesh inside gives when pressed, about
fifteen minutes.

Temper separately: heat two spoons of oil, add mustard seeds and wait for them
to pop, then curry leaves, then a pinch of asafoetida. Pour the whole thing
into the pot and cover it immediately — the aroma escapes in about four
seconds and the lid is the only way to keep it.

Do not add the tempering earlier. Every version where the spices boil with the
stew tastes flat, and the difference is larger than it has any right to be.

## Wet-season kitchari

One cup rice, half a cup split mung, washed together until the water runs
clear. Four cups water, one spoon ghee, half a spoon turmeric, a thumb of
ginger sliced thin.

Cook covered on the lowest flame that maintains a simmer, forty minutes,
without stirring. Stirring makes it gluey, and gluey kitchari is the reason
people think they dislike kitchari.

It should collapse when spooned, not pour. If it pours, cook it uncovered for
five minutes; if it stands up, add hot water a quarter cup at a time.

This is the dish for the second day of a fever, or the third day of rain.

## Banana stem thoran

Peel the outer layers from a foot of banana stem and slice the core into thin
rounds, dropping each into water with a spoon of buttermilk as you go — it
oxidises within a minute otherwise and turns an unappetising brown.

Drain, then cook with grated coconut, one chopped shallot, a green chilli and
a quarter spoon of cumin, covered, on a low flame, ten minutes. No added
water: the stem gives up plenty.

## Pepper rasam

Crush a spoon of black peppercorns and half a spoon of cumin coarsely. Do not
powder them.

Simmer them in three cups of tamarind water with a crushed garlic clove, two
tomatoes and salt until the raw edge goes off the tamarind, about twelve
minutes. Finish with coriander leaves off the heat.

The measure of a rasam is whether it clears the sinuses within two spoonfuls.
If it does not, the pepper was powdered.

## Fried plantain, two ways

**Sweet:** slice ripe plantain on the diagonal and fry in ghee until the sugars
catch at the edges. Salt while hot. The salt is not optional.

**Savoury:** slice unripe plantain into rounds, toss in turmeric and salt, and
fry in oil hot enough that a test piece sizzles immediately. Drain on paper.
Eat within ten minutes — these are excellent hot and deeply mediocre cold.

## Coconut chutney

Half a fresh coconut grated, two green chillies, a thumb of ginger, a spoon of
roasted gram, salt, and enough water to let the blade turn. Grind coarse.

Temper with mustard seed and curry leaf as above.

Keeps four hours at room temperature in the wet season and less if the day is
warm. This is not a make-ahead recipe, and versions that claim otherwise are
describing a different, sadder chutney.

## Notes on the two-burner problem

With two burners, sequence is the whole skill. The order that works for a full
meal:

1. Rice or kitchari goes on first and occupies one burner throughout.
2. The stew or rasam uses the second burner for most of its time.
3. The thoran goes on the second burner after the stew comes off.
4. Tempering for both happens last, in the same small pan, stew first.

Frying plantain breaks this entirely, which is why it is a separate meal
rather than a side.

## On substitutions

Tamarind can be replaced with lime juice at roughly a third of the volume,
added off the heat rather than simmered. It is not the same, and anyone who
grew up with tamarind will say so, but it works.

Curry leaves have no substitute. Omit them and make a different dish rather
than a worse version of this one.

## Storage in humidity

Ground spices last weeks rather than months once the air is wet. Buy small
quantities and accept the higher unit price; the alternative is a large jar
that is stale for most of its life.

Salt clumps. A few grains of raw rice in the pot keeps it pourable and is the
only trick in this book that works exactly as advertised.

Anything fried loses its crispness within twenty minutes regardless of how it
is stored. Plan the meal around that rather than fighting it.
